Build Your Own Checks

validations, data-quality rules and freshness checks

Duration: half a day · Builds on: Pipelines

Your pipeline loads data every week. A check tests that data: is every field filled, is nothing in there twice, is the copy recent? Checks test the data; watching whether the system itself still runs is Monitoring, one of the two frames. A wrong number looks the same as a right one until something checks it.

You leave with

  • Five data-quality checks on the data from your pipeline, written as small queries you can read: missing fields, duplicates, impossible values, a stale date.
  • A red/green list that shows, per check, how many rows fail and which ones.
  • One freshness check that fails loudly when the data is older than you allow.
  • For every red result, a decision: the data is wrong, or the check is.

Ideas to try

  • A bakery checks that every supplier invoice matches an order, and sees which delivery was billed twice.
  • An installer checks that every quote has a site address, a price and an expiry date before it goes out.
  • A coach checks the client list for duplicates and finds the same person under a work and a private email.
  • A restaurant checks that no supplier price rose more than 10% since last month.
  • A web shop checks that every product on the site still has stock in the warehouse export.

Your turn

Which number in your business do you check by hand every week? Bring it; it becomes your first check.

What we do in the session

  1. 30 min — Write down what must be true about your data: every order has a customer, no invoice twice, nothing older than a week.
  2. 60 min — Turn five of those into checks with the AI, each a short query with a name and a count of failures.
  3. 30 min — Run them all and put the result on one red/green list.
  4. 30 min — Look at every red result together: is the data wrong, or is the check wrong? Fix the one that is.
  5. 30 min — Add the freshness check and make the whole set run with one command, after the pipeline.

What you bring

  • The pipeline and data from the Pipelines level, or one export you know well.
  • Two mistakes you have found by hand in that data before, such as a double customer or an invoice without a date.

Tools

DuckDB (free) for the checks as queries; Claude Code to write them; a plain list or spreadsheet for red and green. Optional: the header fields you chose at the Memory level.

Example from my own system

Every booking in my bookkeeping should have a receipt, and every receipt a booking. A check matches the two on date and amount and gives two short lists: bookings without a scan, and scans without a booking. Those two lists are the to-do; the rest matched. Along the way, one field said a booking had an attachment for every one of 11,587 bookings. That is not believable, so the field is not used until it is understood. See Every booking linked to its scan.

Pitfalls

  • A perfect result. 100% green on the first run usually means the check tests nothing. Break a row on purpose and see if it turns red.
  • Letting the same step check its own work. The import that wrote the data is the wrong place to judge it. Count against the source, not against the previous run.
  • No freshness check. Data can be complete, clean and twenty months old. One of my copies was (The mirror that went stale and nobody noticed).

Works best after

Build Your Own Pipelines (level 4). Checks run on data that arrives the same way every time.

Concepts behind it

  • Reality Check — Test the model and the plan against the real world — the actual data, deliveries and behavior — not against the assumption you started with.
  • Never Marks Its Own Homework — The judge must be separate from the builder, and the score is the off-switch: a loop only stops when an independent evaluator says the rubric is met.
  • Rules You Can Verify — '2-space indent' is a rule; 'format code properly' is a wish. A rule the system can't check is decoration.
  • A Confident Error Has No Tell — A hallucination arrives in exactly the same shape as a correct answer. That is the whole problem — and why fluency is not evidence.
