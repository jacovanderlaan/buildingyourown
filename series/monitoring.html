Build Your Own Monitoring

See what your system is doing, and notice when it stops.

Duration: half a day · Builds on: Pipelines · Checks · Automations

Monitoring is the second frame: it watches every level. Checks test whether your data is right. Monitoring watches whether the system runs at all: did the import run, did the job finish, is the service up. A job that fails quietly looks exactly like a job that worked.

You leave with

  • A list of everything that runs in your system (imports, scheduled jobs, services), each with how often it should run.
  • A definition of "working" per item as a measurement: last run, rows written, a page that returns real content.
  • A status page or a daily digest that shows each item as green or red.
  • One alert on your phone when something stops, tested by breaking it on purpose.

Ideas to try

  • A web shop gets a message before opening time when the nightly stock sync did not run, instead of hearing it from a customer.
  • A restaurant sees on Friday at five that the booking widget on its site has stopped answering.
  • An accounting firm gets a red line in the morning digest when a client's bank feed has not delivered for three days.
  • A physiotherapy practice notices that yesterday's appointment reminders were never sent.
  • A one-person business gets one line a day: backup ran, invoices sent, nothing stuck.

Your turn

Which job in your business would you only notice had stopped when a customer complained?

What we do in the session

  1. 30 min — List what runs in your system and how often each part should run.
  2. 40 min — Define "working" for each item as something you can measure. A running process is not enough.
  3. 50 min — Build the status page or daily digest that reads those measurements.
  4. 30 min — Connect one alert to your phone for the item that would hurt most if it stopped.
  5. 30 min — Stop something on purpose, and time how long it takes before you are told.

What you bring

  • Your laptop and the imports or jobs you built at the earlier levels, or a list of the tools you depend on.
  • The last time something stopped without anyone noticing, and how you found out.

Tools

Claude Code to write the checks and the digest; a free monitoring tool such as Uptime Kuma for services; a push app such as ntfy for the alert. Optional: your Memory's daily note as the place where the digest lands.

Example from my own system

My monitoring tool ran for eleven days with zero monitors. It looked installed and watched nothing. Today it checks 19 services in four groups, where possible on the service's own health address instead of its front page, sends alerts to my phone, and feeds a status page that another site reads. In its first week with all 19 in place, it recorded one outage: two minutes, recovered by itself. How I log what breaks is in Incident management that turns every failure into a rule.

Pitfalls

  • Green is not working. A page that answers with a status code can still serve an error. Measure the content or the output, not only that something replies.
  • A monitor that watches nothing. Mine ran empty for eleven days. Check that the monitor itself has something to watch, and that its alert reaches you.
  • Too many alerts. If every small thing pings your phone, you will mute it. Alert on what needs you; put the rest in the daily digest.

Works best after

Pipelines, Checks and Automations. Once things run without you, you need a way to know they still do.

Concepts behind it

  • Silence Is Not Absence of Failure — No complaints does not mean no problems. It usually means people adapted to the errors and stopped mentioning them.
  • Uptime Is Not Correctness — Monitoring proves the system ran. Observability for AI has to prove the answer was right — and those are different instruments.
  • Data Observability — Continuous visibility into the health of your data pipelines — freshness, volume, schema drift, distribution and quality — so problems surface on their own, at the boundary, in time to fix them.
  • Escalate by Exception — Route the easy work to the cheap path and reserve the expensive one for what actually needs it. Power by exception, not by default.

Use cases

Incident management that turns every failure into a ruleEvery disruption — a failed import, a broken page, a dispute with a supplier — becomes a small file with an owner, a status and a lesson. The lesson becomes a rule, so the same failure does not come back.

Resources

  • Extreme Ownership — If you build it, you own it — including the import that silently stopped running.
  • Verify on fresh output — Verify on fresh output, not on what the script printed. Check the artefact, not the log line claiming it is there.
