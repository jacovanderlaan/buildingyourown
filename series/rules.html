Rules · Level 9

Build Your Own Rules

The standing instructions your AI reads before it acts, written from your own mistakes.

Duration: half a day · Builds on: Memory · Skills

Rules tell the AI and your team what to always do and never do. The AI reads them at the start of every task, so a correction you made once does not have to be made again.

You leave with

  • A rules file with 5–10 rules, each from a mistake you or your AI actually made. In Claude Code this file is called CLAUDE.md.
  • Every rule written so it can be checked.
  • One decision record: a choice you made, why, and what you turned down.
  • An agreed place where the next mistake goes, so it becomes the next rule.

Ideas to try

  • A web shop writes down once that orders after Wednesday 2 pm never get a Friday delivery date, and the AI stops promising one.
  • A roofing company records why it stopped quoting very small flat roofs, so nobody reopens the question every spring.
  • A physiotherapy practice writes the rule for which client data may go into an AI tool and which may not.
  • A restaurant keeps one rule file for the AI that writes its menu texts: allergens always listed, no prices in the description.
  • A one-person accountant writes that a closed year is never corrected by hand, and the AI proposes the correction in the next period instead.

Your turn

Which correction have you given your AI, or a colleague, more than twice? Bring the last three.

What we do in the session

  1. 30 min — Collect the corrections you gave over the past few months: in chats, in mail, on sticky notes.
  2. 40 min — Turn each into a rule that can be checked. "Format the quote properly" is a wish; "every quote has a valid-until date" is a rule.
  3. 30 min — Put the rules in the rules file, and run one real task with and without them.
  4. 30 min — Write one decision record for a choice you keep explaining.
  5. 20 min — Agree where the next mistake goes and who turns it into a rule.

What you bring

  • A laptop and three to five corrections you gave, with the situation they came from.
  • One decision you keep having to explain again.

Tools

Claude Code, which reads CLAUDE.md before every task; any text editor. Optional: a folder for decision records in your Memory.

Example from my own system

I went through my own chat history with AI and counted the corrections I had made: 186. In 58% of them the AI had been wrong. In 23% I had never told it what it needed to know. The rest were changed minds, mine or another traveller's, and a world that changed. Most of the wrong answers were things the AI could have known: the preference was written down, but not applied when it mattered. The 186 became 8 fixes: five new written rules about how I travel, a profile of where I like to stay, and a check that loads those rules before every travel suggestion. My rules file did not grow by 186 lines. My incident log works the same way, one rule per failure: see Incident management that turns every failure into a rule.

Pitfalls

  • Rules nobody can check. "Be careful with customer data" changes nothing. "Never paste a customer's bank account number into a chat" does.
  • A new rule for every slip. Group them. Ten rules that get read beat a hundred that get skipped.
  • Rules without a reason. Add one line on why. Without it, the next person deletes the rule the first time it is in the way.

Works best after

Build Your Own Memory, so the rules have a place to live, and Build Your Own Skills, so you have tasks the rules can steer.

Concepts behind it

  • CLAUDE.md as Operating System — Standing rules the AI reads before it acts — a layered OS for how the assistant behaves in your world.
  • The Mistakes File — Same mistake twice = a new rule. Corrections you'd otherwise repeat forever get written into a file the AI reads every run.
  • Rules You Can Verify — '2-space indent' is a rule; 'format code properly' is a wish. A rule the system can't check is decoration.
  • Rules as Data — Constraints and logic expressed as data, not buried in code — rules say what must hold, versioned and enforceable, so quality and governance travel with the model.

Use cases

Incident management that turns every failure into a ruleEvery disruption — a failed import, a broken page, a dispute with a supplier — becomes a small file with an owner, a status and a lesson. The lesson becomes a rule, so the same failure does not come back.Keep the accounting package, own the booking rulesThe official books stay in the accounting package. The knowledge of how things are booked moves out of people's heads into a readable rule file, and a mirror of the books makes them queryable.
