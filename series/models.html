Build Your Own Models

A small map of what your business is made of, so your notes and your data share one shape.

Duration: half a day · Builds on: Memory

Memory gives your documents a place. A model says what the things in them are: a customer, an order, a job, an agreement, and how they hang together. An AI that knows your model stops guessing what "client" means in your files.

You leave with

  • A data model of your business: 5–8 entities (customer, order, product, job, agreement …) with their relationships, as a diagram you can print.
  • For each entity, the 3–6 fields you really use, written down as the header fields of your notes in Memory.
  • Each entity matched to a proven pattern, so you know what you left out and why.
  • Five real records from your own work that fit the model, and one that did not, with the change it caused.

Ideas to try

  • A flower shop models customer, order, delivery address and occasion, and sees that one customer sends to many addresses.
  • A roofing company finds that a quote, a job and a warranty are three stages of the same thing, and models it once.
  • A home care team models client, care plan, visit and caregiver, so a visit always knows which plan it belongs to.
  • A small hotel separates the guest from the booking, and stops losing returning guests in a list of reservations.
  • A freelance translator models client, project and invoice, and adds the language pair as a field instead of a folder.

Your turn

Which word in your business means two different things, depending on who says it? Bring it; that is where the model starts.

What we do in the session

  1. 30 min — Write down the nouns of your business from your own documents: who you work for, what you sell, what you deliver, what you sign.
  2. 40 min — Match each noun to a proven pattern (party, product, order, agreement, work effort, location, event) and decide what you keep.
  3. 40 min — Draw the relationships: one customer, many orders; one job, many visits. The AI draws, you correct.
  4. 40 min — Turn each entity into a short list of fields and add them to the note headers you made in Memory.
  5. 30 min — Test the model on five real records. Fix the model where a record does not fit, not the record.

What you bring

  • The three lists your business runs on: customers, orders or jobs, and products or services, as a spreadsheet or export.
  • Two documents that cross those lists, such as a quote and the invoice that followed it.

Tools

Claude for the first draft of the model and the diagram; a whiteboard or paper for the arguments; your Memory notes from level 1. Optional: a diagram tool such as draw.io, and DuckDB if you want to generate tables from the model straight away.

Example from my own system

When my CRM subscription came up for renewal, I did not move to another CRM. I modeled the work itself. A project, a meeting, a deal and a to-do are all one entity, an activity, which can contain other activities. That idea comes from a classic data-modeling pattern, not from me. The mirror underneath now holds 7,574 people, 4,106 companies and 992 projects, and once the model was right, the screens on top were cheap: AI generated them. See Replacing a CRM before the renewal date and A data model in an afternoon, from a proven pattern.

Pitfalls

  • Modeling the tool instead of the work. Your current CRM or spreadsheet has columns you never use. Start from what happens in your business, not from the export.
  • Inventing what already exists. Customers and suppliers are parties, contracts are agreements. Start from the pattern and leave things out; do not start from a blank page.
  • Twenty entities on day one. Eight you understand beat twenty you have to explain. Add an entity when a real record needs it.

Works best after

Build Your Own Memory (level 1). The model gives the notes you made there their fields and their links.

Concepts behind it

  • Proven Patterns — Proven shapes you reach for instead of inventing — a pattern captures a repeatable solution (Data Vault, dimensional, temporal) so you compose from known-good parts.
  • Model the Process, Not Just the Model — Model not just the data model and the transformations, but the whole process — delivery, integration, migration, quality checking, the backroom — and how the system changes over time.
  • Model the Meaning You Need — The enterprise ontology that models everything ships nothing. Grow shared meaning one high-value use case at a time — and leave batch_id alone.
  • Meaning Before Mechanism — A semantic model is not a technology. The same meaning can ship as a graph, an API, a data product or a contract — decide what it means before deciding what to buy.
