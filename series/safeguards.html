Build Your Own Safeguards

Encryption, a backup you have restored, and access per person: is it safe?

Duration: half a day · Builds on: Memory

Encryption, a backup you have restored, and access per person. Safeguards is a frame: it applies to every level in the series. The more your system knows about your business, the more it matters who can read it and whether you can get it back after a bad day.

You leave with

  • Encryption switched on for the disk or folder that holds your Memory, with the key written down somewhere other than that disk.
  • One backup that has run and has been restored to a new folder, with the file counts compared.
  • An access list as a file: who may read which part, and who may change it.
  • A visibility field on your notes (private, team, public) that anything you publish respects.

Ideas to try

  • A jeweler keeps customer measurements and photos encrypted, and only the owner can open the folder with prices.
  • A roofing contractor restores last week's backup once a month to a spare laptop, and checks that the quotes open.
  • A dental practice gives the front desk access to appointments and the dentists access to treatment notes, each in its own folder.
  • A café shares the staff rota with everyone and keeps the cash counts private, by one field in each note.
  • A freelance designer marks client work as never-share, so it cannot end up in the portfolio by accident.

Your turn

If your laptop were stolen tonight, what would you lose, and who would be able to read it?

What we do in the session

  1. 20 min — List what is valuable in your system and who may see each part.
  2. 30 min — Switch on encryption and store the key where you will find it in a year.
  3. 50 min — Set up one backup, to a second disk or a storage service, and run it.
  4. 40 min — Restore that backup into a new folder. Count the files on both sides and open three of them.
  5. 40 min — Write the access list, add the visibility field to your note header, and test that a private note stays out of a shared folder.

What you bring

  • Your laptop, an external disk or a storage account for the backup.
  • A list of the people who work with your files, and what each of them needs.

Tools

Your laptop's built-in encryption (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on a Mac); a backup tool such as restic or the one your storage service gives you; Claude to write the access list and the checks. Optional: Syncthing to keep a copy on a second device you own.

Example from my own system

My photos pass through a daily pipeline before anything leaves the house. Each new photo is filed by day and activity, and the rules written on its folder classify it as private, shared, needs review or never-share. Only then is it copied to network storage where viewers can read it. The pipeline tracks 206,092 files, runs in dry-run mode by default, and 35 tests guard the rules. See A photo pipeline that knows what is private.

Pitfalls

  • A backup you have never restored. Two of my own backups stopped for five to six days without a single message. Only a restore, or a count, tells you a backup works.
  • The key next to the lock. An encryption key saved on the same disk is no key. Write it down where a fire or a thief will not reach both.
  • One switch for everything. Cloud services often offer share-all or share-nothing. Your business has more categories, so put the choice in a field on each note.

Works best after

Memory. Start as soon as your files are in one place, and come back to it before anyone else gets access, at the Control Plane level at the latest.

Concepts behind it

  • Guardrails Are Layers — A guardrail is not one filter at the end. It's seven checkpoints with different jobs — and the one everyone skips is memory.
  • Rules You Can Verify — '2-space indent' is a rule; 'format code properly' is a wish. A rule the system can't check is decoration.
  • Hooks Over Hopes — Every instruction to a model is a probability, not a promise. Rules that must always run belong in deterministic automation the model can't skip.
  • One-Way Publishing — You don't sync systems — you publish from one. Sync is where rot starts.
