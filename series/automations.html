Data · Level 6

Build Your Own Automations

One job that runs on a schedule and does the work without you.

Duration: half a day · Builds on: Pipelines · Checks

Pipelines move data; automations do work with it: write the weekly summary, send the reminder, sort the inbox, file the photos. The job runs whether you remember it or not, and it tells you when it needs you.

You leave with

  • One scheduled job that does a real task for your business, such as a weekly summary, a reminder or a sorted list, and runs without you.
  • A dry-run mode, so you can see what the job would do before it does it.
  • A signal that it ran: a line in a log or a note in your Memory with the date and what it did.
  • A message when it fails or finds something that needs a person, and silence when it does not.

Ideas to try

  • A garden center gets a Monday list of plants that sold out last week, ready for the order.
  • A painting company sends every customer a reminder two days before the job, from the planning export.
  • A care agency gets a Friday overview of visits that were planned but not signed off.
  • A holiday rental owner gets a note the evening before each arrival with the guest, the time and the open questions.
  • A one-person accountant gets a daily list of clients whose documents are late, and nothing else.

Your turn

Which task do you do every week that is exactly the same as last week? Bring the last three times you did it.

What we do in the session

  1. 30 min — Pick the job and describe "done" in two sentences: what goes in, what comes out, who reads it.
  2. 60 min — Build the job with the AI and run it by hand on your own data until the result is right.
  3. 30 min — Add the dry-run: the job shows what it would do, and only acts when you say so.
  4. 30 min — Put it on a schedule (the task planner on your laptop, or a small server) and let it run once while we watch.
  5. 30 min — Add the signal that it ran, and the one message that comes only when something needs you.

What you bring

  • The task you chose, with three real examples of its result: three weekly summaries, three reminder mails.
  • The data the task needs, ideally from a pipeline you built at the Pipelines level.

Tools

Claude Code to write the job; Python; the scheduler your computer already has (Task Scheduler on Windows, cron on a Mac or a small server). Optional: a mail or chat connector for the message, from the Integrations level.

Example from my own system

Every morning a job reads the day's mail and sorts it with the decisions I made once per sender, about 1,380 of them. My daily note then shows a short triage section. On a typical day, 95 mails became one that needed me. The decisions are the asset; the job applies them every morning without asking. See From 95 mails to the one that needs you. My photo pipeline works the same way: it runs every day at 07:30, with dry-run as the default (A photo pipeline that knows what is private).

Pitfalls

  • No news is taken as good news. A job that stops makes no noise. Five of six accounts at one of my banks had not synced for months before I noticed. Build the alarm before you trust the automation.
  • Acting before looking. Run in dry-run until you have seen three correct results, then switch it on.
  • Messages for everything. A job that reports every success trains you to ignore it. Report the exception.

Works best after

Build Your Own Pipelines (level 4) and Build Your Own Checks (level 5). An automation that works on unchecked data does the wrong work faster.

Concepts behind it

  • Hooks Over Hopes — Every instruction to a model is a probability, not a promise. Rules that must always run belong in deterministic automation the model can't skip.
  • Escalate by Exception — Route the easy work to the cheap path and reserve the expensive one for what actually needs it. Power by exception, not by default.
  • Silence Is Not Absence of Failure — No complaints does not mean no problems. It usually means people adapted to the errors and stopped mentioning them.
  • Compounding Efficiency — Spend time, money and attention where they compound — cut the meetings, the licensed bloat and the unstructured chat that add nothing, and put the resource into structure that pays back.

Use cases

Resources

  • Compounding — Small consistent returns beat large occasional ones, given time. What matters is the number of deposits, not the size of one.
  • Systems, not goals — A goal is reached and then leaves you without a next move; a system keeps producing.
