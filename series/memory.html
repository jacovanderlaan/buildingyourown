Structure · Level 1

Build Your Own Memory

Your notes, documents and decisions, ordered so you and your AI can find them.

Duration: half a day

This is level 1: everything else in the series stands on it. An AI that cannot find your last offer will write a new one from scratch.

You leave with

  • Your own domain as a handful of folders, one for each part of your business, with the same four sub-folders in each.
  • 10–20 real notes from your own work, each with a small header (date, type, customer, status) that an AI can read.
  • A day structure: one folder per day for receipts, scans and meeting notes, so a date is always enough to find something.
  • One search that finds a note by customer, date or type, in seconds.

Ideas to try

  • A bakery keeps every supplier invoice in the folder of the day it arrived, so "what did we pay for flour in March?" is one search.
  • An installer gives every job one note with the address, the quote, the photos and the warranty date.
  • A coach keeps one note per client with the sessions underneath, and the AI prepares the next session from it.
  • A web shop files every returned order with the reason in the header, and sees after a month which product comes back most.
  • A one-person consultancy writes each proposal as a note with status: sent / won / lost, and knows its hit rate without a CRM.

Your turn

Which document do you look for most often and find last? Bring three examples of it.

What we do in the session

  1. 20 min — Map your business in four to six areas, and give each the same four sub-folders: plans, active work, history, insights.
  2. 30 min — Choose the header fields that matter to you (date, type, customer, status) and write them down as your first rule.
  3. 60 min — Turn 10–20 of your own documents into notes with that header. The AI does the typing; you check every one.
  4. 30 min — Set up the day structure and move receipts or scans into it.
  5. 20 min — Ask your memory five real questions and see what it finds, and what it misses.

What you bring

  • A laptop and 10–20 real documents: offers, invoices, meeting notes, a client list.
  • One question you would like your own records to answer.

Tools

Obsidian (free) for the notes; Claude for the conversion; your own laptop folders. Optional: a scanning app on your phone.

Example from my own system

Every receipt, scan and meeting note in my system lands in the folder of its day. That one convention holds 4,289 receipts that I can find by date, and it is why the accounting questions further up the series can be answered at all: a booking, its receipt and the day they belong to sit next to each other. See Every receipt filed on the day it happened.

Pitfalls

  • Starting with the tool. The folders and the header come first; Obsidian, SharePoint or a shared drive all work once the structure is clear.
  • Too many fields. Four fields you always fill beat twelve you sometimes fill.
  • Importing everything at once. Start with what you use this month; history can follow.

Works best after

This is the first level. Everything else builds on it.

Concepts behind it

  • Document Vault — Plain files in a deliberate shape that both you and AI can navigate.
  • The Calendar Is the Spine — One time-axis is the backbone of a second brain — everything dated hangs off it.
  • Metadata First — Metadata is the material Breakthrough Modeling is built from — structure, logic and meaning captured as data, so the platform and the AI can both read and run it.
  • Company Brain — The org-scale second brain: shared, structured, permission-aware knowledge AI can rely on. Wiring tools together isn't it — access is not memory.

Resources

  • Getting Things Done — The original inbox discipline: everything gets captured, clarified and given one next action. Email triage starts here.
  • Zettelkasten — One idea per note, each note linked to its neighbours. The structure is what the links turn out to be.
