Start With a Tiny Experiment, Not a Project

Design thinking for the first AI tool in a small company

By Jaco van der Laan

Most small companies that want "something with AI" start with the wrong unit. They write a plan, pick a platform, and ask for a quote. Three months later there is a pilot that nobody uses. The unit should have been an experiment small enough to fail on a Tuesday afternoon.

The question comes before the tool

Design thinking has one rule I keep coming back to: understand the real need before you choose the solution. For an AI tool that means one sentence, written before anything else: what takes time, goes wrong, or lives in one person's head?

"We want AI for our customer contact" is not that sentence. "Every Monday someone spends two hours finding which quotes have had no answer for two weeks" is. The second one can be built. The first one can only be bought.

Make the experiment small enough to lose

An experiment has a fixed size and a fixed end. Anne-Laure Le Cunff calls them tiny experiments: a trial with a duration, not a goal. For a first AI tool I use three limits:

  • One question. Not a dashboard, one answer.
  • One data source. One export, one folder, one mailbox.
  • One afternoon. If it needs more, the question is too big.

When I wanted to know which relationships I was letting go cold, I did not start by replacing my CRM. I took my own mail headers, counted interactions per person, and looked at the list. It was wrong in an interesting way: a job board that had sent me almost thirty thousand mails was my "strongest relationship". That one wrong answer told me more about the design than a week of planning would have. The fix — separate one-to-one mail from bulk mail — became the most useful factor in the score.

Read the result honestly

An experiment is only useful if you are allowed to stop. Before you start, decide what result would make you drop it. Then look at the result, not at how much you liked building it.

Some of mine stopped. A second library in a desktop asset manager held 80,752 items, and every one of them was a copy, because the app cannot point at files it does not own. The experiment was meant to test the app for inspiration images. The result was clear, and I left the app. That is a successful experiment: cheap, fast, and decisive.

Others are still running. A purchase history built from order-confirmation mails works for the two largest shops and is not finished for the rest. It stays an experiment until it earns a screen.

Then decide: drop, repeat or build

After an experiment there are three honest outcomes:

  1. Drop it. The question was not worth it, or the data is not there. You lost an afternoon, not a budget.
  2. Repeat it with one thing changed: another data source, another rule, another person's question.
  3. Build it. Only now does it become a project, with a spec, rules and checks — because now you know what you are building.

I play with a new building block on my own data first, prove it there, and only then apply it in a business. That order matters. By the time something reaches a client or a colleague, it has already failed a few times where failing was free.

What this looks like on a Build Day

A Build Day is one experiment per participant. The questionnaire and the call before the day cut each case down to one question, one data source and one afternoon. At the end of the day there is a demo, and the demo is the result you read honestly. Some participants take home a tool. Some take home a clear "not like this" — and the next experiment.

Both are good outcomes. The bad outcome is the three-month pilot nobody uses.

Use cases in this article

  • A relationship score from your own mail and messages
  • A purchase history from order confirmations
  • Leaving an asset manager that copies everything

Concepts behind it

  • Design Thinking — Approach modeling as design: understand the real need, explore options, and experiment fast — and with AI you can now try new methods, build applications and test them at a speed that was impossible before.
  • Fail Fast — Set experiments up to fail quickly and cheaply, read the signal honestly, and move on — don't linger in something that, in the end, may not work.
  • The Experiment Loop — Play with a hypothesis: hypothesize → experiment → learn → decide. The mechanism that turns tinkering into a proven block.
  • Play Personally → Prove the Block → Apply in Business — The flow from personal experiment to business application.
